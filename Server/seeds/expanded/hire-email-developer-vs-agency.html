HTML email is its own skill. It is not the same as building web pages. Email clients like Outlook still use old rendering engines, Gmail strips some CSS, and dark mode can flip your colors. So the question is not only "who is cheapest?" but "who can ship emails that work everywhere, on time, again and again?" This guide walks through the three options, what to ask, and the warning signs.

Option 1: A Freelance Email Developer

A good freelancer can be excellent value. You talk directly to the person writing the code, and prices are often lower than an agency's.

Works well when: you need one template, a quick fix or a small batch, and the deadline is flexible.

Watch out for: holidays, illness or a busy month can stall your project. Skill levels vary a lot. Some freelancers are web developers who also "do email", and they may not know Outlook quirks or dark mode fixes. Ask how they handle Outlook rendering problems before you hire.

Option 2: An In-House Email Developer

A full-time developer knows your brand, your platform and your calendar. They can react the same day.

Works well when: you send many campaigns each week, run complex flows and have enough work to fill a full role.

Watch out for: total cost includes salary, benefits, testing tools and training. Hiring takes time, and if that one person leaves, the knowledge can leave with them. Testing tools that preview emails in many clients are usually a paid subscription on top.

Option 3: An Email Development Agency

An agency gives you a team: developers, QA and often designers. Work is usually priced per template or per month.

Works well when: you need steady output without a full-time hire, you work across several platforms, or you have deadlines around launches and sales.

Questions to Ask Any Option

  1. Can I see live examples that work in Outlook and on mobile?
  2. Which email clients do you test in? Outlook, Gmail and dark mode should be included. See our testing checklist.
  3. Will the templates be editable in my platform?
  4. What is the turnaround and how many revisions are included?
  5. Who owns the code, and do you offer support after delivery?
  6. How do you handle accessibility, such as alt text, contrast and readable font sizes?
  7. How do you keep file size small enough to avoid Gmail clipping?

Red Flags

  • Templates built with divs, flexbox or a single image.
  • No mention of Outlook or testing.
  • Very low prices with no revisions or support.
  • Code generated by AI and not tested. See AI-generated email templates.
  • No portfolio of real, sent emails, only design mockups.
  • Vague answers about who owns the final code.

A Simple Test Task

If you are unsure, start with one paid template. Give every candidate the same design and brief. When it comes back, check:

  • Does the layout use tables, for example <table role="presentation" width="100%">, rather than divs for structure?
  • Do buttons still work when images are off? A bulletproof button uses a real link with styled text, not an image.
  • Is there a hidden preheader text line?
  • Does it stack properly on a phone?
  • Does it look right in dark mode and in classic Outlook for Windows?
  • Can your team edit text and images in your platform without breaking it?

This one test tells you more than any sales call.

A Real-World Scenario

A growing online store has a marketer who builds campaigns in a drag-and-drop editor. Black Friday is coming, and the team wants a custom modular template, a new abandoned cart design and a fixed order confirmation. The workload is too big for the marketer, but not big enough for a full-time hire. A freelancer is booked up for November. In this case an agency makes sense: a fixed scope, a fixed deadline, testing included and people available if something breaks the week of the sale. After the season, the store can drop back to occasional work.

Cost Thinking

Compare total cost, not only the quote. A cheap template that breaks in Outlook costs you in lost clicks and extra fixes. An in-house hire costs salary even in quiet months. An agency costs more per hour than a freelancer, but often less than a full-time role, and it removes the single-point-of-failure risk.

How to Brief Whoever You Hire

A clear brief saves time and revisions with any option. Include:

  • The design file, such as Figma, with desktop and mobile views.
  • The platform the template will live in, such as Klaviyo, Mailchimp or HubSpot.
  • Which parts must be editable by your team, and which should be locked.
  • Brand fonts, colors and any fallback fonts you accept.
  • The email clients that matter most to your audience.
  • Your deadline and who will approve the final version.

If your design team works in Figma, share our Figma to HTML email handoff checklist with them first. It covers widths, spacing, image exports and the notes a developer needs. A good developer or agency will also ask questions back. If nobody asks about Outlook, dark mode or editing, treat that as a warning sign.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to hire an email developer?

Freelance rates and agency prices vary widely. Custom templates commonly range from about $40 for simple builds to several hundred dollars for complex modular systems.

Is it better to hire a freelancer or an agency for email templates?

A freelancer suits small one-off jobs. An agency suits ongoing work, tight deadlines and projects that need broad testing and platform knowledge.

What should an email developer deliver?

Tested, responsive, table-based HTML that is editable in your platform, plus test screenshots and support for fixes.

When does an in-house email developer make sense?

When you have enough steady email work to keep one person busy full-time, and budget for testing tools and training.

Can a web developer build my HTML emails?

Some can, but email needs different techniques, such as table layouts and inline styles. Ask for tested email examples before you hire.
